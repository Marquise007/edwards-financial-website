How the adjustment works

Claiming before full retirement age reduces the benefit by five ninths of one percent for each of the 36 months immediately before it, then five twelfths of one percent for each month earlier than that. For someone with a full retirement age of 67, claiming at 62 is a 30 percent reduction, and it is permanent.

Claiming after full retirement age earns delayed retirement credits of two thirds of one percent per month, which is 8 percent a year, for anyone born in 1943 or later. Credits stop at 70. There is no reason to wait beyond that.

What this does not model: the retirement earnings test if you claim early while still working, taxation of benefits, Medicare premiums deducted from the payment, spousal and survivor benefits, and cost of living adjustments. For a married couple the survivor benefit usually argues for delaying the higher earner regardless of what the breakeven shows.

Delayed retirement credits are not earned on spousal or survivor benefits.

Source: Social Security Administration full retirement age schedule, and Congressional Research Service report R47151 on adjustment factors for early and delayed claiming. This is general information and not advice on when to claim. Confirm your own figures at ssa.gov.
