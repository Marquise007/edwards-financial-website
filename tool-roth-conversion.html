What this does and does not do

A Roth conversion moves money from a tax deferred account to a Roth account. The converted amount is ordinary income in the year of conversion. After that it grows and, if requirements are met, comes out free of federal income tax, and it is not subject to required minimum distributions during the original owner's lifetime.

This models federal ordinary income tax only. It does not include state income tax, the 3.8 percent net investment income tax, capital gains, the alternative minimum tax, the additional standard deduction for taxpayers 65 and older, credits, or phase outs.

Two costs a bracket chart does not show. A conversion raises modified adjusted gross income, which can lift Medicare Part B and Part D premiums two years later, and can increase the share of Social Security benefits that is taxable. Both can make a conversion that looks efficient on rates alone cost more than expected.

Conversions are permanent. Recharacterisation of a conversion has not been available since 2018. Paying the tax from outside the account rather than from the conversion itself is what makes the arithmetic work in most cases.

Where a traditional IRA holds both deductible and non deductible money, the pro rata rule applies across all traditional IRAs and only part of the conversion is taxable. This model assumes the full amount is taxable.
